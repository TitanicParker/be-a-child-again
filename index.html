A relational education project

Be a Child Again

What if children have been rehearsing the structure of intelligibility all along—in puzzles, games, toys, stories, rules, repairs, choices, and play—and adult life is a specialization of those same relational acts?

Everyone is a beginner here. Not because the subject is obscure, but because we do not normally grow up with an ordinary vocabulary for noticing how something becomes intelligible enough to continue from.

Human beings repeatedly make situations more determinate. We distinguish. We open possibilities. We establish relations. We repair what does not hold. We organize parts into wholes. We settle what has remained live.

The formal theory calls these Transformation Patterns within the Constraint Grammar of Completed Intelligibility. But this project begins before the formal vocabulary. It begins with what children already do.

The child already lives in structure.

Children find, fit, match, sort, hide, search, balance, join, separate, build, correct, choose, take turns, follow, respond, invent rules, repair rules, finish puzzles, and begin again.

A global play survey began with 188 candidate structural descriptions and consolidated them into 97 recurring play types. Its early recurring forms included finding and revealing, fitting and connecting, matching and distinguishing, ordering and sequencing, transforming, stabilizing and controlling, pursuing and avoiding, imitating and coordinating, and pretending.

Play repeatedly places children in small, revisable worlds in which noticing relations and acting on them changes what can happen next.

A jigsaw is a world in which local relations have to become sufficient for a larger whole to appear. A maze is a world of openings, blocked continuations, alternatives, dead ends, and successful passage. A tower makes support, dependence, instability, adjustment, and enoughness visible. Pretend play reveals that what something can mean and do depends on the relational field in which it is taken up.

The adult has not left that world behind.

A separate survey of human work began with 260 candidate activity descriptions and consolidated them into 76 structural work activities. A surgeon and a mechanic diagnose. A nurse and a farmer monitor. An editor and an auditor verify. A carpenter and a software integrator assemble. A teacher and a physician explain. A chef, nurse, conductor, and logistician coordinate.

The objects differ. The standards differ. The consequences differ. The knowledge and ethical responsibilities differ. Yet the structural activity can remain recognizable.

Most strikingly, after independently constructing its taxonomy, the work survey recovered six deeper operation families: differentiate; make available or open access; establish a consequential relation; restore adequacy; integrate into a functioning whole; settle a live demand.

Children may already be rehearsing the elementary material from which adult intelligible action is composed.

That changes the case for teaching.

A child speaks before learning grammar, handles quantity before learning arithmetic notation, hears rhythm before learning music theory, and navigates space before learning geometry. Education often gives explicit form to capacities already in use.

The same may be possible for intelligibility. Children already encounter moments in which a difference becomes decisive, a possibility becomes available, two things are seen to bear on one another, something wrong becomes right enough, parts acquire places in a whole, or a live question becomes settled enough to continue.

What they generally do not receive is a shared vocabulary for recognizing those events across contexts.

This is why the subject is not “the 54” as a list to memorize. The subject is how intelligibility happens. The 54 are a proposed vocabulary for recurring forms in which local intelligibility becomes complete enough to remain available.

This.
This, not that.
From here.
This bears on that.
Set right.
One whole.
Arrived.

These can first be encountered in pictures, games, stories, disagreements, constructions, puzzles, and ordinary speech. Formal coordinates can come later.

The educational aim is not classification for its own sake. It is to help a person notice what is happening in a situation and become more deliberate about the kind of response that would actually meet it.

A sentence can become inspectable in a new way.

We already inspect sentences for spelling, grammar, syntax, truth, and style. We can also ask what a sentence does to a shared situation.

Did it distinguish? Did it open a way forward? Did it establish the relation that mattered? Did it repair a misunderstanding? Did it gather the parts into one intelligible whole? Did it settle something that was genuinely ready to be settled? What remains for the other person after the sentence lands?

That is not merely language instruction. It is education in the consequences of making things intelligible to one another.

The sentence gives us the larger reorientation.

A sentence can be described as a string of individual words. That is true and radically incomplete. The sentence becomes what it is because the words bear on one another. Position, contrast, dependence, sequence, and expectation create a coherent whole that no inventory of isolated words contains.

The words remain distinguishable. But what each word is doing here depends on the community of the sentence.

Human life can be viewed from the same angle.

We are used to beginning with individual people and then adding relationships. But much of a person's intelligible life is relational from the beginning. Teacher, child, friend, rival, witness, neighbour, patient, judge, citizen, guest, colleague, parent, stranger: these are positions of bearing.

A person is distinguishable, but not intelligible in isolation from the fields of bearing in which that person stands.

Language is inherited through others. Roles exist with respect to others. Promises persist because others can claim them. Institutions coordinate expectations. Domains preserve previous work. Professions are constituted partly by standards, responsibilities, and relations of uptake.

The relational turn does not abolish the individual. It makes individuality more exact.

Looked at relationally, life is not less personal. It is more structured.

A common underlying literacy

If intelligibility can be taught as a literacy, then a puzzle, a disagreement, a science experiment, a paragraph, a proof, a game, a story ending, and a group project no longer have to appear as wholly separate cognitive worlds. They can become places where children recognize recurring forms of change.

A learner can move from:

I can do this.

I can see what kind of thing I just did.

I can recognize the same structure somewhere else.

I can deliberately offer that structure to another person.

At that last stage, education becomes explicitly social. Understanding is not only something a person possesses. It is something people can help one another reach.

One person's completed intelligibility can punctuate another person's wonder without ending wonder itself. A distinction can be offered. A relation can be shown. A route can be opened. A repair can be made. A whole can be gathered. A question can be allowed to rest.

Then life continues from different ground.

Why Be a Child Again?

The name is not nostalgia. It is methodological humility.

No generation has been raised with an ordinary shared language for the proposed forms of completed intelligibility. Whatever one's age or expertise, one enters this field as a beginner.

Childhood may be where the structure is easiest to see because the phenomena are still close to the surface: the block fits or does not; the tower stands or falls; the hidden thing is found; the pieces come together; the rule works or breaks the game; the story reaches somewhere; the other child understands or does not.

We may have been learning these forms in play, performing them in work, accumulating them in domains, and transmitting them in sentences for as long as there has been a human world.

The activity may be ancient. The explicit vocabulary may be new.

Look again at what we have always been doing.
